# 01 — UK Pensions Primer

A plain-English map of how UK retirement income is built. *(Rules and figures move
each tax year — those below are labelled by year and should be verified on
[GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/) before relying on them.)*

## The three layers of UK retirement income

1. **State Pension** — a flat-rate income from the government, based on your
   National Insurance (NI) record.
2. **Workplace / personal pensions** — pots you and your employer build up, or a
   guaranteed income from an older-style scheme.
3. **Other savings & assets** — ISAs, cash, investments, property.

Most plans combine all three.

## 1. State Pension

- **New State Pension** (for those reaching State Pension age after April 2016).
- Full rate **2025/26: ~£230.25/week ≈ £11,973/year**. *(2026/27 figure rises under
  the "triple lock" — confirm current rate on GOV.UK.)*
- Needs **35 qualifying years** of NI for the full amount; **at least ~10 years**
  to get anything. Fewer years = a proportionate amount.
- **State Pension age** is currently **66**, rising to **67** between 2026–2028, and
  to **68** thereafter. Check Janey's exact age at
  [gov.uk/state-pension-age](https://www.gov.uk/state-pension-age).
- **Check her record & forecast:** [gov.uk/check-state-pension](https://www.gov.uk/check-state-pension).
  Gaps can sometimes be filled with voluntary NI contributions — often excellent value.

## 2. Workplace & personal pensions

Two families:

### Defined Contribution (DC) — a pot
- You + employer pay in; it's invested; the **pot value at retirement is what you get**.
- The vast majority of pensions today. Value depends on contributions, growth, charges.
- **Auto-enrolment minimum** for employees: **8% of qualifying earnings** (at least
  **3% employer**, the rest from employee + tax relief). Many employers pay more.
- Types: workplace scheme, **personal pension**, **SIPP** (self-invested, wide choice),
  **stakeholder**. The self-employed use personal pensions / SIPPs (no employer top-up).

### Defined Benefit (DB) — a guaranteed income
- Older "final salary" / "career average" schemes pay a **guaranteed annual income**
  for life, usually inflation-linked. Valuable — rarely worth transferring out.
- If Janey has one, it's modelled as income, not a pot.

## 3. Tax — why pensions are efficient

- **Tax relief on contributions:** you get relief at your marginal rate. A basic-rate
  taxpayer paying £80 has £100 in the pension (20% relief); higher-rate taxpayers can
  claim more via self-assessment.
- **Annual Allowance:** most people can contribute up to **£60,000/year** (2025/26)
  or **100% of earnings if lower** with tax relief. Unused allowance can sometimes be
  carried forward 3 years.
- **Money Purchase Annual Allowance (MPAA):** drops to **£10,000/year** once you
  flexibly access a DC pot — a trap to avoid if still contributing.
- **Tax-free cash:** normally **25% of the pot tax-free** at retirement, capped by the
  **Lump Sum Allowance (£268,275)**. The rest is taxed as income when drawn.
- **Access age:** normally **55, rising to 57 from April 2028**.
- The old **Lifetime Allowance was abolished** (April 2024) and replaced by lump-sum
  allowances.

## Turning a pot into income (at retirement)

- **Drawdown:** keep the pot invested and draw from it flexibly. Common rule of thumb:
  **~4%/year** as a sustainable starting rate (not a guarantee).
- **Annuity:** exchange the pot for a **guaranteed income for life**. Certainty, less
  flexibility. Rates vary with age, health, and interest rates.
- **Mix:** e.g. annuitise enough to cover essentials, keep the rest in drawdown.
- **UFPLS / lump sums:** take chunks, 25% of each tax-free.
